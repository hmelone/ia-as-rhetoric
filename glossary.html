Glossary of Key Terms

The following terms appear throughout this site and are central to understanding how information architecture functions as rhetorical practice. Definitions draw on primary sources cited in the References.

Codification Cognitive Walkthrough Ethos Faceted Classification Hierarchical Classification Information Architecture Rhetorical Practice Tacit Knowledge Taxonomy
Codification
The process of making organizational knowledge explicit, structured, and accessible, transforming it from tacit know-how into documents, databases, or system categories. Davenport and Prusak describe codification as the effort to put knowledge “into a form that makes it accessible to those who need it,” making it “organized, explicit, portable, and easy to understand as possible.” The challenge, as they note, is doing so without stripping knowledge of its distinctive meaning: “some structure for knowledge is necessary, but too much kills it.” See also: Tacit Knowledge, Taxonomy.
Cognitive Walkthrough
A structured usability evaluation method in which evaluators simulate how a user would step through an interface to complete a task, assessing whether each step provides adequate navigational cues and feedback. Used by Matloobtalab and Ferati to assess how users interpreted and navigated a university self-service portal. The method is particularly useful for identifying structural failures, moments where users cannot determine where they are, where to go next, or whether their actions have succeeded. See: Usability.
Ethos
In classical rhetoric, the appeal to credibility and trustworthiness. In the context of information architecture, ethos refers to the degree to which a system’s structural consistency, predictability, and coherence signal to users that it is reliable and authoritative. When structural cues fail and users cannot determine where they are or whether their actions have succeeded, ethos collapses, and users lose confidence in the system’s basic reliability. See also: Rhetorical Practice; Usability.
Faceted Classification
A classification system in which each unit of content is associated with multiple metadata categories, enabling it to appear in different indexes and support multiple simultaneous navigational pathways. Unlike strict hierarchy, which assigns each item a single parent and position, faceted systems allow users to approach content from different conceptual directions. Ruzza et al. advocate for hybrid models that pair hierarchical structure for stable content with faceted classification for dynamic material. The rhetorical significance of faceted classification is that it distributes interpretive control, accommodating multiple user perspectives rather than encoding a single authoritative view. See also: Hierarchical Classification; Structure.
Hierarchical Classification
A structural model that organizes content through parent-child relationships, determining what is general and what is specific, what is primary and what is subordinate. Hierarchy encodes a particular interpretive logic. Content placed high in the structure is implicitly more important or general; content buried in subsections is implicitly secondary or difficult to reach. This is not a neutral ordering. It assigns meaning by position. When applied to complex, heterogeneous content that resists a single organizational logic, hierarchy can produce navigational confusion and structural inconsistency. See also: Faceted Classification; Structure.
Information Architecture (IA)
Rosenfeld, Morville, and Arango define information architecture as “the art and science of shaping information products and experiences to support usability, findability, and understanding.” In this site’s argument, IA is understood as more than a technical discipline, but also a form of rhetorical practice. The structural decisions that constitute an IA, including classification, taxonomy, hierarchy, navigation, and labeling, are persuasive acts that shape what users see, how they interpret relationships between concepts, and whether they trust what they find. IA is not a passive scaffold for content; it is a set of design choices that actively constructs the conditions under which knowledge is encountered. See also: Rhetorical Practice; IA & Rhetoric.
Rhetorical Practice
In technical communication scholarship, rhetorical practice refers to design and communication activities that actively shape the social and interpretive conditions of communication, not merely describe or organize content. Michael Salvo argues that information architecture should be understood as “a site of intervention,” where practitioners engage and change the world by shaping the systems and environments within which content is encountered and interpreted. This framing positions the information architect as an active rhetorical agent rather than a neutral technician. See also: Information Architecture; IA & Rhetoric.
Tacit Knowledge
Know-how, experiential understanding, and contextual judgment that resides within practitioners and resists full explicit representation. Davenport and Prusak observe that tacit knowledge “incorporates so much accrued and embedded learning that its rules may be impossible to separate from how an individual acts.” When tacit knowledge is codified for inclusion in a knowledge system, it is necessarily transformed. The richness of the original is reduced to what the system’s structural categories can represent. This transformation is a rhetorical act, shaped by organizational vocabulary, hierarchical relationships, and the distinctions the system makes available. See also: Codification; Codification.
Taxonomy
A hierarchical classification system that organizes concepts, terms, or content into categories and subcategories. In knowledge management, Dalkir describes organizational taxonomies as driven not by objective first principles but by consensus: “the purpose of an organizational taxonomy is not to come up with a universally accepted way of describing reality.” Rather, taxonomies encode the organizational values and conceptual priorities of particular communities. The implication for IA is that taxonomies are rhetorical constructs. They shape what knowledge workers can search for, find, and make sense of within a system. Koenig’s formulation “taxonomy before technology,” quoted by Dalkir, captures the priority: taxonomic choices are more consequential than the tools used to implement them. See also: Hierarchical Classification; Codification.
