Glossary of Key Terms

The following terms appear throughout this site and are central to understanding how information architecture functions as rhetorical practice. Definitions draw on primary sources cited in the References.

Codification Cognitive Walkthrough Ethos Explicit Knowledge Faceted Classification Hierarchical Classification Information Architecture Ontology Rhetorical Practice Tacit Knowledge Taxonomy
Codification
The process of representing organizational knowledge in a form that can be stored, shared, and used. Davenport and Prusak describe codification as putting knowledge into an organized, explicit, portable, and understandable form. Codification requires decisions about what to preserve, how much context to include, which terminology to use, and which relationships or distinctions to make explicit. See also: Explicit Knowledge; Tacit Knowledge.

Why this matters: Codification determines what knowledge becomes available for later organization, search, navigation, and retrieval.

Cognitive Walkthrough
A structured usability evaluation method in which evaluators simulate how a user would step through an interface to complete a task, assessing whether each step provides adequate navigational cues and feedback. Used by Matloobtalab and Ferati to assess how users interpreted and navigated a university self-service portal. The method is particularly useful for identifying structural failures, moments where users cannot determine where they are, where to go next, or whether their actions have succeeded. See: Usability.

Why this matters: The method gives us a way to see, step by step, where structural failures produce confusion. It turns abstract claims about ethos into observable user behavior.

Ethos
In classical rhetoric, the appeal to credibility and trustworthiness. In the context of information architecture, ethos refers to the degree to which a system’s structural consistency, predictability, and coherence signal to users that it is reliable and authoritative. When structural cues fail and users cannot determine where they are or whether their actions have succeeded, ethos collapses, and users lose confidence in the system’s basic reliability. See also: Rhetorical Practice; Usability.

Why this matters: Ethos is what makes a knowledge system credible. When ethos collapses, the failure registers as distrust, not inconvenience, and users seek alternatives they believe.

Explicit Knowledge
Knowledge that has been articulated and recorded in a form that others can access and use, such as a procedure, definition, specification, rule, document, or database record. Because explicit knowledge has been represented in a persistent form, it can be stored, shared, classified, searched, and incorporated into a knowledge system. See also: Tacit Knowledge; Codification.

Why this matters: Explicit knowledge provides the representations that a knowledge system can organize, connect, and retrieve.

Faceted Classification
A classification system in which each unit of content is associated with multiple metadata categories, enabling it to appear in different indexes and support multiple simultaneous navigational pathways. Unlike strict hierarchy, which assigns each item a single parent and position, faceted systems allow users to approach content from different conceptual directions. Ruzza et al. advocate for hybrid models that pair hierarchical structure for stable content with faceted classification for dynamic material. The rhetorical significance of faceted classification is that it distributes interpretive control, accommodating multiple user perspectives rather than encoding a single authoritative view. See also: Hierarchical Classification; Structure.

Why this matters: Facets give users multiple paths to the same content. Where hierarchy privileges the designer’s view, facets accommodate the reader’s.

Hierarchical Classification
A structural model that organizes content through parent-child relationships, determining what is general and what is specific, what is primary and what is subordinate. Hierarchy encodes a particular interpretive logic. Content placed high in the structure is implicitly more important or general; content buried in subsections is implicitly secondary or difficult to reach. This is not an inevitable ordering. It assigns meaning by position. When applied to complex, heterogeneous content that resists a single organizational logic, hierarchy can produce navigational confusion and structural inconsistency. See also: Faceted Classification; Structure.

Why this matters: No hierarchy is inevitable. Each privileges a single interpretive path and assigns meaning by position, which is why the choice of hierarchy is itself a rhetorical decision.

Information Architecture (IA)
Rosenfeld, Morville, and Arango define information architecture as “the art and science of shaping information products and experiences to support usability, findability, and understanding.” In this site’s argument, IA is understood as a form of rhetorical practice because structural decisions influence the conditions under which knowledge is encountered. Classification, taxonomy, hierarchy, navigation, and labeling shape what users see, how they interpret relationships between concepts, and whether they trust what they find. See also: Rhetorical Practice; IA & Rhetoric.

Why this matters: This is the central term of the site. Understanding IA as rhetorical practice establishes the foundation for the arguments that follow.

Ontology
An ontology is a semantic model that defines the relationships between concepts and entities in a domain, going beyond a taxonomy’s classification of items into categories. Where a taxonomy says what something is a kind of, an ontology can also express how concepts connect, such as what causes, requires, or is compatible with what. Because an ontology decides which concepts exist and how they relate, it carries the same rhetorical weight as any other structural choice. These models are increasingly machine-readable, which means they shape not only human understanding but also how AI systems retrieve, recommend, and reason over information. See also: Taxonomy; Structural Models.

Why this matters: As knowledge systems feed machines as well as people, the relationships encoded in an ontology determine how both interpret a domain, making it a rhetorical decision with growing reach.

Rhetorical Practice
In technical communication scholarship, rhetorical practice refers to design and communication activities that actively shape the social and interpretive conditions of communication. Michael Salvo argues that information architecture should be understood as “a site of intervention,” where practitioners engage and change the world by shaping the systems and environments within which content is encountered and interpreted. This framing positions the information architect as an active rhetorical agent. See also: Information Architecture; IA & Rhetoric.

Why this matters: Salvo’s reframing changes the stakes. If IA is rhetorical practice, designers are responsible for the interpretive work their structures perform.

Tacit Knowledge
Know-how, experiential understanding, and contextual judgment that are difficult to articulate fully. Davenport and Prusak observe that tacit knowledge can incorporate so much accumulated experience that its rules may be difficult to separate from how an individual acts. Capturing tacit knowledge therefore requires people to articulate and select aspects of what they know before that knowledge can be represented in a system. See also: Explicit Knowledge; Codification.

Why this matters: Some of the knowledge most useful to others depends on experience and context. How that knowledge is captured influences what later readers and systems have available to interpret.

Taxonomy
A controlled vocabulary for classifying concepts, terms, or content. Taxonomies can be flat lists of approved terms, allow items to belong under multiple parents, or take the most common form: a hierarchical tree of categories and subcategories. In knowledge management, Dalkir describes organizational taxonomies as driven not by objective first principles but by consensus: “the purpose of an organizational taxonomy is not to come up with a universally accepted way of describing reality.” Rather, taxonomies encode the organizational values and conceptual priorities of particular communities. The implication for IA is that taxonomies are rhetorical constructs. They shape what knowledge workers can search for, find, and make sense of within a system. Koenig’s formulation “taxonomy before technology,” quoted by Dalkir, captures the priority: taxonomic choices are more consequential than the tools used to implement them. See also: Hierarchical Classification; Codification.

Why this matters: Taxonomies are negotiated, not discovered. They reflect what an organization believes matters, and they determine what users can find.
