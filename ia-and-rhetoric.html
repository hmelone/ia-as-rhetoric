Part One

Information Architecture as Rhetorical Practice

Every structural decision in a knowledge system, from category names to hierarchical placements to navigation paths, is an act of meaning-making. Organization is not neutral. It teaches users how to read the content it holds.

The Instability of Information

Rosenfeld, Morville, and Arango define information architecture as “the art and science of shaping information products and experiences to support usability, findability, and understanding.” Information, they argue, lives in the messy middle between raw data and actionable knowledge. It is not facts to be stored and retrieved, and it is not yet understanding. It requires a receiver to make sense of it, and that receiver brings their own context, vocabulary, and assumptions to the encounter.

This has direct consequences for how an architect must work. If two receivers of the same content can arrive at different interpretations, then the architect’s job cannot be to find the one correct way to organize content. There is no neutral arrangement waiting to be discovered. Every category name, every hierarchical placement, every navigation path foregrounds some relationships and obscures others, and a different architect making different choices would influence how users understand the same content. Structure does not just describe meaning. It shapes how meaning gets made.

Davenport and Prusak make the same point from the knowledge management side. Knowledge, they argue, “originates and is applied in the minds of knowers.” It does not sit inside a document waiting to be retrieved. A user constructs it in the moment of use, out of what the document supplies and what they already bring to it. The implication for any knowledge system is the same as Rosenfeld’s, but it adds an active dimension. The system cannot deliver meaning. It can only create the conditions under which a user constructs meaning.

The relevant question is therefore not whether structure matters but who shapes it, on what terms, and toward what ends.

Salvo’s Intervention: IA as a Site of Intervention

If meaning is constructed rather than delivered, then the structure surrounding information becomes consequential. Someone must decide how content is organized, labeled, and connected, and those decisions shape how users interpret what they find. Information architecture is the practice of making those decisions. Thinking of the practice as organization emphasizes one part of that work — arranging materials for findability and access. Thinking of it as rhetoric emphasizes another — the interpretive consequences those arrangements carry. The two are complementary. Michael Salvo has given the rhetorical side its sharpest formulation. Information architecture, he argues, is “a site of intervention,” not a service performed on someone else’s content. The architect is not only arranging objects. They are also shaping the conditions under which knowledge can be encountered.

Practitioners have sometimes treated structural work as a functional task best measured by efficiency and findability. A site can be efficient and still rhetorically misleading. It can be findable and still distort what is found. Salvo treats IA as a practice that engages and changes the world by shaping the contexts within which content is understood, not by adding to the content itself.

Information architecture is not simply a method for organizing information. It is a practice that shapes how information is understood.

Where you put a piece of content, what you name it, how visible you make the path to it — those choices direct the user’s attention and shape what they expect to find. Users do not usually notice these choices were made. They just feel the result.

A small example clarifies the point. Consider a single help article on resetting an account password. Placed under Account Settings, the article reads as routine maintenance, the kind of housekeeping any user might perform on a quiet afternoon. Placed under Security, the same article reads as a defensive measure, an action taken in response to risk. Placed under Troubleshooting, it reads as evidence that something has gone wrong. The content remains unchanged, but the structure surrounding it alters how users interpret its purpose and relevance.

The Three Classical Appeals

Looking at information architecture as a rhetorical practice, the classical rhetorical appeals provide a useful framework for understanding how structure shapes meaning. Aristotle distinguished three appeals through which speakers and writers persuade their audiences: logos, ethos, and pathos. These three devices map onto how IA constructs meaning:

Logos: the appeal to logic and reason. In IA, logos lives in the hierarchy, in how concepts relate to one another, and in the navigation paths the system makes available. These choices present a logic of how knowledge fits together. They guide users toward particular inferences about which ideas belong with which, and which are subordinate to which.

Ethos: the appeal to credibility and trust. Ethos lives in consistency and predictability. When labels behave the same way across the site, when navigation does what it appears to promise, when categories match what they contain, the system signals authority and earns trustworthiness. When those signals are inconsistent, the appeal collapses.

Pathos: the appeal to emotion and feeling. When users cannot interpret a system’s structure, the result is confusion, frustration, uncertainty, and eventually abandonment.

Design Implications

Designing a knowledge system involves more than picking a taxonomy, labeling categories, and arranging menus. The structure you build will do interpretive work for every user who arrives at the system after you. Most of those users will never see the decisions that shaped what they read. That makes the architect responsible for the meaning the structure carries, whether or not anyone asked them to be.

And that work begins earlier than many realize. Information architecture does not begin with menus or navigation or search. It begins with the prior decisions about what knowledge will be represented in the system at all.
