Part One

Information Architecture as Rhetorical Practice

The delivery of information cannot be treated as objective. Every structural decision in a knowledge system, from category names to hierarchical placements to navigation paths, is an act of meaning-making, not merely of organization.

The Instability of Information

Rosenfeld, Morville, and Arango define information architecture (IA) as "the art and science of shaping information products and experiences to support usability, findability, and understanding." Their more consequential observation, however, concerns the nature of information itself: it "lives in the messy middle" between raw data and actionable knowledge. It is not data, which can be stored and retrieved as discrete objective facts, yet it is not quite knowledge either. It requires a receiver to make sense of it.

This instability has profound design implications. As Rosenfeld et al. observe, "No document fully and accurately represents the intended meaning of its author. No label or definition totally captures the meaning of a document. And no two readers experience or understand a particular document or definition or label in quite the same way." If no label fully captures what a document means, then every structural decision in a knowledge system becomes an act of meaning-making, not merely of organization.

"No label or definition totally captures the meaning of a document. And no two readers experience or understand a particular document in quite the same way." — Rosenfeld, Morville & Arango

Davenport and Prusak's account of knowledge reinforces this from a knowledge management perspective. Knowledge, they argue, is "a fluid mix of framed experience, values, contextual information, and expert insight" that "originates and is applied in the minds of knowers." The user constructs knowledge through interpretation, and the system shapes that interpretation by determining what information is visible, how it is grouped, and what relationships are implied by its placement. Structure precedes meaning.

Salvo's Intervention: IA as a Site of Intervention

The field of technical communication has developed a robust account of IA that makes this rhetorical dimension explicit. Michael Salvo argues that information architecture should be understood as "a site of intervention," where practitioners move beyond description and analysis to actively shaping the technocultural systems through which communication occurs.

For Salvo, IA is not a passive arrangement of existing content but an act of design that constructs the social and interpretive conditions of communication. Drawing on Kaufer and Butler's conception of rhetoric as a "design art," Salvo frames IA as a practice that "engages and changes the world" not by adding content, but by shaping the contexts within which content is understood.

This challenges a persistent assumption in IA practice: that organizing information is primarily a functional task, properly evaluated by metrics of efficiency and findability. Salvo's intervention is to insist that structural decisions are never merely functional. They reflect and reinforce particular interpretive lenses.

The Three Classical Appeals

The three classical rhetorical appeals map onto IA's structural operations in ways that clarify its persuasive character:

Logos: The appeal to logic and reason. In IA, the organization and hierarchy of information constitute a form of logical argument. They present relationships between concepts in ways that appear ordered, guiding users toward particular inferences about how ideas connect.

Ethos: The appeal to credibility and trust. Consistency in labeling and predictability in navigation signal that a system is coherent, authoritative, and worthy of trust.

Pathos: The appeal to emotion and feeling. When structural cues are unclear or inconsistent, the result is confusion, frustration, and disengagement. These are affective responses produced by structural failure. That they arise precisely when structure fails confirms that structure was doing rhetorical work all along.

"The information architect is not a neutral technician who organizes content for efficiency. Rather, the architect is an active rhetorical agent who constructs the conditions under which users engage." — After Salvo, 2004

The placement of categories, the specificity of labels, and the visibility of certain pathways over others all function rhetorically by directing attention and shaping expectations. These decisions can privilege certain interpretations while obscuring others, reinforcing particular ways of understanding information without users being aware that a choice was made at all.

Design Implications

Salvo's reframing of the designer's role carries direct implications for practice. The information architect is not a neutral technician. Recognizing the rhetorical dimension of structural decisions is the first step toward designing knowledge systems that serve users not just efficiently, but meaningfully.
