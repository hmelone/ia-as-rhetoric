Part One

Information Architecture as Rhetorical Practice

At first glance, rhetoric and information architecture appear to belong to different disciplines. One is typically associated with language and persuasion, the other with organization and usability. Yet both ask the same fundamental question: how do the choices we make shape understanding?

Rhetoric and the Knowledge Base

Rhetoric may seem like an unusual framework for understanding information architecture. It is usually associated with speeches, writing, and persuasion rather than navigation, taxonomy, or metadata. Yet rhetoric provides a vocabulary for examining how choices about arrangement, emphasis, and relationship influence interpretation.

Rhetoric is the study of how communication shapes understanding. At its core it asks a practical question: How do the choices a communicator makes lead an audience toward one interpretation rather than another? Those choices include word selection and tone, but also arrangement, emphasis, and the relationships created between ideas.

A knowledge system is any structured collection of content built so people can find and use it, like a help center, a product documentation portal, an internal wiki, or a self-service support site. These systems do more than store articles. They arrange them, label them, and connect them in ways that influence how information is understood. Every one of those decisions is communicative. This is where information architecture and rhetoric begin to converge.

This perspective is consistent with both information architecture and knowledge management scholarship. Rosenfeld, Morville, and Arango define information architecture as the art and science of shaping information products to support usability, findability, and understanding. Davenport and Prusak add the knowledge-management view: knowledge “originates and is applied in the minds of knowers.” Information can be stored, but understanding is constructed in use. A knowledge system, then, cannot hand someone understanding. It can only create the conditions in which understanding is built.

The arrangement is never separate from the content. It is part of the message.

A Framework: The Three Classical Appeals

Aristotle described three appeals through which communication persuades an audience: logos, ethos, and pathos. Although he developed these appeals to explain persuasive speech, they also provide a useful vocabulary for understanding how information architecture communicates.

Logos is the appeal to logic. In a knowledge system, logos lives in the hierarchy and the navigation paths. The way you group and nest topics presents an argument about how the subject fits together, and it steers readers toward conclusions about which ideas belong with which, and which sit beneath which.

Ethos is the appeal to credibility. Ethos lives in consistency. When labels mean the same thing everywhere, when a link goes where it says it will, and when a category actually contains what its name promises, the system reads as reliable. When those signals contradict each other, credibility erodes.

Pathos is the appeal to emotion. In a knowledge system, pathos is mostly the feeling the structure produces in use: the quiet confidence of a site that makes sense, or the rising frustration of one that does not. When readers cannot interpret a structure, that frustration ends in abandonment.

These three are a lens, not a checklist. The sections that follow return to them whenever a structural decision is doing rhetorical work.

There Is No Single Right Structure

If two readers can take the same article and arrive at different interpretations, there is no single correct way to organize content waiting to be discovered. Every category name and every placement makes some connections visible and renders others invisible. That is how structure works rhetorically. A different architect making different choices would lead readers to understand the same content differently. Structure does not just describe meaning. It shapes how meaning gets made.

A single example makes this concrete. Take one help article on resetting an account password. Filed under Account Settings, it reads as routine maintenance. Filed under Security, the same article reads as a response to risk. Filed under Troubleshooting, it reads as a sign that something has gone wrong. The words on the page never change, however, the category around them changes what readers think the article is for. The placement of the article creates important context. That is logos at work.

Unlike writing, information architecture often operates below the level of conscious attention. Readers rarely notice the structure itself, but they strongly experience its effects.

IA as a Site of Intervention

If structure influences interpretation in these ways, information architecture cannot be understood as a neutral organizational activity measured only by findability. A site can be efficient and still misleading, findable and still distorting what is found. Michael Salvo says that information architecture is “a site of intervention,” not a service performed on someone else’s content. The architect is not just arranging objects. They are shaping the conditions under which knowledge gets encountered.

What This Means for Your Work

The structure you build does interpretive work for every reader who arrives after you, and almost none of them will see the decisions behind it. That makes information architects responsible for the meaning their structure carries.

That responsibility starts earlier than the navigation menu. Before you can arrange content, someone has to decide what content exists in the first place, including what knowledge gets written down, what remains tacit, and how that knowledge is represented.
