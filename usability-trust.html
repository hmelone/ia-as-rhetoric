Part Four

When IA Fails: Usability, Trust, and the Collapse of Ethos

The rhetorical side of information architecture is easiest to see when the architecture fails. When structural cues are unclear or inconsistent, readers lose the ability to interpret the system, and their confidence in what it tells them goes with it.

The earlier parts argued that architecture shapes meaning. Codification sets what a system can express, and structural models shape how readers encounter it. This section turns to what happens when those organizing principles break down. What breaks with them is not speed but credibility, and people walk away unsure whether the system can be trusted at all.

When Structure Stops Communicating

A study by Matloobtalab and Ferati shows the dynamic in concrete terms. They examined a university self-service portal, the kind students and faculty use to submit requests, pull documents, and handle administrative tasks. Combining expert review, observed task-testing with real users, and follow-up interviews, they looked at how people interpreted and moved through the portal’s structure. One pattern recurred. Users found the interface short on navigational cues, unable to convey its own organizing logic.

One participant observed that the system was “not really coherent … not very straightforward … You do not get any clues in the interface.” Another expressed ongoing uncertainty about whether actions had been correctly completed: “I don’t know, like now I’ve done it 5 minutes ago. I still do not know where to put it [the request]. I have not learned.”

A third participant named the distrust outright. “My only thought when using those kinds of websites is that I am not sure if my main goal is to send the message [request] to the correct place.”

A Collapse of Ethos

That third comment is the one worth sitting with. This person is not merely lost. They have stopped trusting that the system does what it claims, with no way to confirm whether their action went through. The cues that would normally signal reliability have failed. This is what a collapse of ethos looks like in a working system.

When ethos goes, the breakdown runs deeper than usability. The system is no longer a credible source. It becomes something the reader has to second-guess at every step, which is exhausting and, eventually, disqualifying.

The Numbers Behind the Feeling

Navigation does more than move people around; it communicates. Every extra click a reader spends hunting for something registers as more than friction. It signals that the system is not holding up its end of the exchange.

The quantitative side of the study bears this out. Matloobtalab and Ferati tracked two measures, how participants rated overall usability and how many clicks each task required. The two moved together in a strong inverse relationship (r = −0.737, p < 0.05). More clicks, lower ratings. The significance is not the clicks themselves. They matter because they signal uncertainty about how the system is organized, and that uncertainty feeds straight into distrust.

Why Abandonment Is the Rational Response

The larger finding is the pointed one. When readers cannot interpret a system’s logic, they leave it for an alternative they trust more. In this study, participants overwhelmingly chose to skip the portal and go straight to IT staff, not because the portal lacked the right content, but because its structure failed to convey that content reliably.

This is Salvo’s “site of intervention” failing in practice. The architecture was supposed to set up a working exchange. When it does not, people do the sensible thing and find a human who can help. You do not fix that portal by adding features. You fix it by giving it a structure people can read.

Structure as Responsibility

Information architecture is a rhetorical system. It shapes how people encounter, judge, and act on information, and across these sources the evidence points one way. Structure does work that matters.

The stakes are rising. Organizations lean harder on self-service documentation every year, and AI systems increasingly rely on structured representations of knowledge for retrieval, recommendation, and reasoning. The semantic models described in Part Three, from taxonomies to ontologies, increasingly shape machine retrieval as well as human navigation. The rhetorical decisions embedded in those models therefore shape not only how people understand a subject but also how intelligent systems interpret it. A retrieval system is only as trustworthy as the architecture feeding it.

Salvo’s framing is, in the end, a call to take responsibility for the interpretive work your structures perform. Recognizing that is where good knowledge systems begin: they serve people efficiently and meaningfully because someone treated their organization as something readers must understand and trust. When that trust goes, so does the system. The failure is rhetorical because information architecture is not merely a container for communication. It is part of the communication itself.
