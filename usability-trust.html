Part Four

When IA Fails: Usability, Trust, and the Collapse of Ethos

The rhetorical character of information architecture becomes most visible when that architecture fails. When structural cues are unclear or inconsistent, users lose the ability to interpret the system at all, and with it, their confidence in what the system tells them.

The earlier sections of this argument showed that structure shapes meaning. Codification determines what a system can express. Structural models determine how users can encounter what has been expressed. This section examines what happens when that structure breaks down. The result is a failure of confidence, of credibility, of trustworthiness. Usability failures register as trust failures, and the metric users carry away is not how many clicks a task required but whether the system can be believed.

When Structure Stops Communicating

A study by Matloobtalab and Ferati illustrates the dynamic concretely. The researchers examined a university self-service portal, the kind of system through which students and faculty submit requests, retrieve documents, and manage administrative tasks. Using a combination of expert evaluation, observed task-testing with real users, and follow-up interviews, they assessed how participants interpreted and navigated the portal’s structure. A consistent pattern emerged across participants — users described the interface as lacking navigational cues and struggling to communicate its organizational logic.

One participant observed that the system was “not really coherent … not very straightforward … You do not get any clues in the interface.” Another expressed ongoing uncertainty about whether actions had been correctly completed: “I don’t know, like now I’ve done it 5 minutes ago. I still do not know where to put it [the request]. I have not learned.”

A third participant put the distrust plainly: “My only thought when using those kinds of websites is that I am not sure if my main goal is to send the message [request] to the correct place.”

A Collapse of Ethos

That last comment is the one worth lingering on. The user is not just lost. They have stopped trusting that the system is doing what it claims to do. They have no way to confirm whether their action succeeded. The structural cues that would normally tell them the system is reliable have failed. This is a collapse of ethos.

When ethos collapses, the relationship between system and user breaks down at a deeper level than usability. The system is no longer a credible source. It is something the user has to second-guess at every step.

Quantitative Confirmation

Navigation is not merely functional. It is communicative. When users have to click repeatedly to find what they need, the extra clicks register as more than friction. They register as a degraded relationship with the system itself.

The quantitative side of the Matloobtalab and Ferati study confirms this. The researchers measured two things: how participants rated the system’s overall usability, and how many clicks each task took to complete. The two moved together in a strong inverse relationship (r = −0.737, p < 0.05). The more clicks a task required, the lower participants rated the system. Excess clicks indicated navigational confusion, and that confusion translated directly into reported dissatisfaction. The numbers describe a communicative failure: when structure stops guiding users, they stop trusting that the system is engaging with them as competent agents.

Abandonment as Rational Response

The broader implication is striking. When a knowledge system’s structural logic cannot be interpreted, users abandon it for alternatives they trust more. Matloobtalab and Ferati found that participants overwhelmingly preferred to bypass the portal entirely and approach IT staff directly, not because the portal lacked the right content, but because its structure failed to communicate that content reliably.

This is what Salvo’s “site of intervention” looks like when it fails. The architecture was supposed to set up the conditions for a working communication. When it does not, users do the only rational thing left: they leave the system and find a person who can actually help them. The portal does not get fixed by adding more features. It needs a structure users can read.

Conclusions: Structure as Responsibility

Information architecture is not a technical background condition for knowledge delivery. It is a rhetorical system that shapes how users encounter, evaluate, and act on information. The evidence across these sources lines up: structure is doing work that matters.

As organizations rely more on self-service documentation, and as AI-driven search and content generation get layered onto these systems, the stakes only get higher. The structures behind a knowledge base now determine not only what users can find, but what they are able to understand and believe.

Salvo’s “site of intervention” framing is a call for designers to take responsibility for the interpretive work their structures perform. Recognizing this is the first step toward designing knowledge systems that serve users not just efficiently, but meaningfully.

When users stop trusting a knowledge system, they stop using it. The failure is not merely technical. It is rhetorical, because structure is communication.
