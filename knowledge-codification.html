Part Two

Knowledge Codification and the Limits of Representation

Before a knowledge base can be structured, someone has to decide what knowledge gets captured and how it will be represented. That step, called codification, shapes what the system can remember, what it can retrieve, and what it can never know.

Codification is the work of transforming knowledge into a representation a system can hold, whether an article, database record, or other structured artifact. It is the stage where knowledge becomes content, long before readers ever encounter it.

Every representation leaves something behind. An article can preserve steps, terminology, relationships, and examples, but it cannot fully preserve experience, judgment, or context. Because every representation is selective, every act of codification also frames the knowledge it presents. That framing is rhetorical. It influences what future readers can know, how they understand a problem, and what they are likely to overlook.

Whatever does not survive codification cannot be organized, found, or read later, no matter how effective the navigation is.

What Codification Actually Involves

Davenport and Prusak describe codification as the effort to put organizational knowledge into a form that is organized, explicit, portable, and easy to understand. Doing that requires more than writing information down. Authors decide what belongs in the article, what context can be omitted, how much detail readers need, and what vocabulary best represents the knowledge. Each decision changes what the system can preserve and what future readers can reconstruct.

Why So Much Knowledge Resists Capture

The knowledge that gets lost most often is what knowledge management calls tacit knowledge. This is the know-how and contextual judgment that lives in a practitioner’s head. Davenport and Prusak distinguish between two forms of knowledge. Explicit knowledge can be written down as a procedure, definition, or rule. Tacit knowledge resists representation because much of what makes it useful depends on experience and context rather than words alone.

The challenge of codification is therefore not simply writing knowledge down. It is deciding how much of that tacit understanding can be represented without losing what made it valuable in the first place.

Consider a support engineer who has just solved a customer issue. In that moment they know what fixed the problem, why it worked, what they ruled out, and how they would recognize the same issue if it appeared in a different situation. Much of that understanding is tacit. As they turn it into an article, they decide which details to preserve, which to omit, how to describe the problem, and which terms future readers are likely to search for. Those decisions determine how the knowledge will be represented, and they shape what future readers will be able to understand from it.

They also decide where the article belongs, perhaps under Authentication, Network Errors, or Common Issues. Classification makes the structure more visible, but it does not create it. Decisions about scope, terminology, examples, and relationships have already begun structuring the knowledge before the article is ever placed into a category.

Other aspects of the information architecture also influence those decisions. Existing taxonomies, templates, metadata, and governance shape how authors represent new knowledge, while each new article, category, and relationship gradually reshapes the architecture itself. Codification and other aspects of information architecture therefore continually influence one another as a knowledge system evolves.

What This Means for Your Work

By the time a reader reaches an article, a long chain of upstream choices has already shaped what they can find, what it is called, what context remains, and what relationships are visible. Those choices live in the codification layer, where the system’s vocabulary and conceptual structure begin to take shape.

The practical takeaway is that codification shapes every part of a knowledge system. Decisions about what knowledge to capture, how to describe it, and how to represent it become part of the architecture that supports navigation, search, metadata, and AI retrieval. Key Terms

Codification The process of making tacit knowledge explicit and accessible through structured representation.
Tacit Knowledge Know-how and experiential understanding that resists full explicit representation.
Taxonomy A hierarchical classification system that encodes organizational values and shapes what can be known.
