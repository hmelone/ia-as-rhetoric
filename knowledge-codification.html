Part Two

Knowledge Codification and the Limits of Representation

Before anyone can organize a knowledge base, someone has to decide what knowledge gets captured and how it will be represented. That step, called codification, determines what the system can remember, what it can retrieve, and what it can never know. Every architectural decision that follows depends on those earlier choices.

Codification is the work of transforming knowledge into a representation a system can hold, whether an article, database record, or other structured artifact. It is the stage where knowledge becomes content, long before readers ever encounter it.

Whatever does not survive codification cannot be organized, found, or read later, no matter how good the navigation is.

These choices matter because of a point introduced in Part One: knowledge is constructed by readers rather than delivered to them. The same article can leave two people with different understandings. Codification is therefore never just transcription. It is a series of judgments about what to capture, what to leave behind, and how the remaining knowledge should be framed. Those judgments travel with the content for the life of the system.

What Codification Actually Involves

Davenport and Prusak describe codification as the effort to put organizational knowledge into a form that is organized, explicit, portable, and as easy to understand as possible. That sounds like a clean technical task. It is not. Deciding what counts as “understandable” forces three smaller decisions: understandable to whom, in what vocabulary, and at what level of detail. Each one reflects what the organization values, not a fact about the knowledge itself.

The harder problem is what gets dropped. Davenport and Prusak put it plainly: some structure for knowledge is necessary, but too much kills it. Too little, and the knowledge stays locked up and unusable. Too much, and it gets flattened into something that no longer carries what made it worth keeping. Every codification decision is a balance between preserving what matters and making it accessible, and that balance is a judgment call.

Why So Much Knowledge Resists Capture

The knowledge that gets lost most often is what knowledge management calls tacit knowledge: the know-how and contextual judgment that lives in a practitioner’s head. Davenport and Prusak split knowing into two kinds. Explicit knowledge can be written down, like a procedure or a definition. Tacit knowledge resists being written down, because so much of what makes it useful is the situation it came from.

Picture a support engineer who has just fixed a customer issue. In that moment they hold a small piece of tacit knowledge: what fixed it, why, what they ruled out, and how they would spot the same problem if it surfaced differently next time. Now they have to write it up. They choose which steps to keep and which to skip, and they pick a title in words other engineers will search for. Those are codification decisions, judgments about what to capture and how to phrase it. They are also rhetorical decisions, because they shape how future readers will understand the problem.

Then they file the article under a category, maybe Authentication, maybe Network Errors, maybe Common Issues. Choosing that category is a classification decision, the first act of structuring, and it happens at the writing desk rather than in a navigation menu.

Codification and information architecture intersect at this moment, which is why the boundary between them is often less distinct than it first appears. Information architecture is usually associated with organizing existing content, but many of the structural decisions that shape a knowledge system have already been made. When writers decide what concepts exist, how they should be described, and what vocabulary the system will use, they are creating the material that information architecture later organizes. In more advanced knowledge systems, those relationships may be formalized through metadata models, taxonomies, or ontologies, but they begin as human judgments about what belongs together and why.

In practice, existing architechture influences codifcation as well. Taxonomies, templates, metadata, and governance shape how authors represent knowledge, while each new article, category, and relationship gradually reshapes the architecture itself. Representation and structure evolve together, each influencing the other over time.

What This Means for Your Work

By the time a reader reaches an article, a long chain of upstream choices has already shaped what they can find: what got captured, what it was called, and what it was filed near. Those choices live in the codification layer, where the system’s vocabulary is set, and they are largely invisible from the front end.

The practical takeaway is that naming is not cosmetic. The categories and terms established during codification are inherited by every navigation system, search result, and AI retrieval process built on top of them. Codification transforms knowledge into representations a system can hold, while information architecture organizes, connects, and presents those representations. In practice, however, the two evolve together, each shaping the other over time. The next section, structural models, looks at how those organizational choices influence interpretation.
