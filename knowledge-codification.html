Part Two

Knowledge Codification and the Limits of Representation

Before knowledge can be organized, searched, or retrieved, it has to be represented in a form the system can hold. Codification is the process of representing knowledge in a form that can be stored and shared. Because every representation is selective, codification is also where some of the rhetorical work of information architecture begins.

What Codification Involves

Davenport and Prusak describe codification as putting organizational knowledge into a form that is organized, explicit, portable, and understandable. In practice, that means deciding what belongs in the representation: which details matter, how much context is necessary, what terminology to use, and which relationships should be made explicit.

These are not simply writing decisions. They begin to establish the vocabulary and conceptual relationships that the rest of the knowledge system will use. What is preserved, omitted, named, or emphasized affects what later readers have available to interpret.

Why Knowledge Resists Capture

Not everything a person knows can be easily represented. Knowledge management distinguishes between explicit knowledge, which can be expressed as a procedure, definition, rule, or other artifact, and tacit knowledge, the experience and contextual judgment that are much harder to put into words.

A support engineer who has just resolved an issue may know what fixed it, why it worked, which possibilities were ruled out, which clues mattered, and how the same problem might appear under different circumstances. Turning that understanding into an article requires choices about what to preserve, what to omit, how to describe the problem, and which terms another person might use to find it.

Some of that knowledge will survive the transition into content. Some will not.

Codification and Information Architecture

Codification does not happen independently of information architecture. Existing templates, metadata, taxonomies, terminology, and governance influence how new knowledge is represented. A required field determines what information an author must supply. A controlled vocabulary constrains how a concept can be named. A template establishes which kinds of information receive emphasis.

The relationship also works in the other direction. New concepts may require new metadata, categories, relationships, or terminology. As a knowledge system grows, the way knowledge is represented and the way it is organized influence one another.

Before a reader navigates a hierarchy or enters a search query, earlier decisions have already shaped what knowledge is available, what it is called, and which relationships have been preserved.

What This Means for Your Work

Information architecture does not begin with the navigation menu. Decisions made while knowledge is captured and represented become part of the architecture that later supports classification, navigation, search, and retrieval.

Key Terms

Codification Representing knowledge in a form that can be stored, shared, and used within a system.
Explicit Knowledge Knowledge that can be expressed and recorded as a procedure, definition, rule, or other artifact.
Tacit Knowledge Knowledge rooted in experience and context that resists full explicit representation.
