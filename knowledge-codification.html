Part Two

Knowledge Codification and the Limits of Representation

Before a knowledge base can be structured, someone has to decide what knowledge gets captured and how it will be represented. That step, called codification, shapes what the system can remember, what it can retrieve, and what it can never know.

Codification is the work of transforming knowledge into a representation a system can hold, whether an article, database record, or other structured artifact. It is the stage where knowledge becomes content, long before readers ever encounter it.

Every representation leaves something behind. An article can preserve steps, terminology, relationships, and examples, but it cannot fully preserve experience, judgment, or context. Because every representation is selective, every act of codification also frames the knowledge it presents. That framing is rhetorical. It influences what future readers can know, how they understand a problem, and what they are likely to overlook.

Whatever does not survive codification cannot be organized, found, or read later, no matter how effective the navigation is.

What Codification Actually Involves

Davenport and Prusak describe codification as the effort to put organizational knowledge into a form that is organized, explicit, portable, and easy to understand. Doing that requires more than writing information down. Authors decide what belongs in the article, what context can be omitted, how much detail readers need, and what vocabulary best represents the knowledge. Each decision changes what the system can preserve and what future readers can reconstruct.

These decisions are rhetorical because they shape the knowledge that readers will later encounter. Choosing what to include, what to omit, how to describe a concept, and what vocabulary to use influences not only what can be found, but what can be understood. Codification frames knowledge before a taxonomy is built, a navigation system is designed, or a search result is returned.

Codification therefore involves continual tradeoffs. Knowledge that is left unstructured cannot easily be shared or retrieved, but knowledge that is overstructured risks losing the context that made it valuable in the first place. The goal is not to simplify knowledge until it becomes thin. The goal is to preserve enough meaning for the representation to remain useful outside the original situation.

Why So Much Knowledge Resists Capture

The knowledge that gets lost most often is what knowledge management calls tacit knowledge. This is the know-how and contextual judgment that lives in a practitioner’s head. Davenport and Prusak split knowing into two kinds. Explicit knowledge can be written down, like a procedure or a definition. Tacit knowledge resists being written down because so much of what makes it useful is the situation it came from.

Picture a support engineer who has just fixed a customer issue. In that moment they hold a small piece of tacit knowledge. They know what fixed it, why it worked, what they ruled out, and how they would recognize the same problem if it surfaced differently next time. When they write it up, they choose which steps to keep, which details to omit, and which words other engineers are likely to search for. Those are codification decisions. They are also rhetorical decisions because they shape how future readers will understand the problem.

Then they file the article under a category, maybe Authentication, maybe Network Errors, maybe Common Issues. Choosing that category is a classification decision, but it is not the beginning of the architectural work. The title, scope, terminology, examples, and omissions have already structured how the problem can be understood. Categorization makes that structure explicit.

Existing information architecture also influences those decisions. Taxonomies, templates, metadata, and governance shape how authors represent knowledge, while every new article, category, and relationship gradually reshapes the architecture itself. Information architecture does not begin after codification, and codification does not happen independently of architecture. The two continually shape one another as a knowledge system evolves.

What This Means for Your Work

By the time a reader reaches an article, a long chain of upstream choices has already shaped what they can find, what it is called, what context remains, and what relationships are visible. Those choices live in the codification layer, where the system’s vocabulary and conceptual structure begin to take shape.

The practical takeaway is that codification shapes every part of a knowledge system. Decisions about what knowledge to capture, how to describe it, and how to represent it become part of the architecture that supports navigation, search, metadata, and AI retrieval. Key Terms

Taxonomy A hierarchical classification system that encodes organizational values and shapes what can be known.
