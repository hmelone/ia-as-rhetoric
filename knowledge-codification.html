Part Two

Knowledge Codification and the Limits of Representation

The rhetorical work of information architecture begins long before a user encounters a menu, search result, or navigation path. It begins with knowledge codification — the work of deciding what knowledge is worth making explicit and how. Those decisions shape what can be known through the system.

Information architecture does not begin with menus, navigation, or search. It begins with decisions about what knowledge will be represented in a system at all. Codification creates the raw material that IA later organizes, and the choices made at this stage constrain everything that follows.

What Codification Does

Davenport and Prusak describe codification as the effort to put organizational knowledge into a form that is “organized, explicit, portable, and easy to understand as possible.” The phrasing sounds straightforward and practical, but the work it describes is not. Deciding what counts as “understandable” means deciding for whom, in what vocabulary, and at what level of detail. Each of those decisions reflects organizational priorities and assumptions rather than objective truths about the knowledge itself.

The harder problem is what gets lost. As Davenport and Prusak put it, some structure for knowledge is necessary, but too much kills it. Too little structure and the knowledge remains inaccessible. Too much and it is flattened into something that no longer conveys what was meaningful about it. The codifier is making judgments about how to preserve what matters while making the knowledge available.

The Tacit/Explicit Distinction

What gets lost most often is what knowledge management calls tacit knowledge — the know-how, experiential understanding, and contextual judgment that lives inside practitioners. Davenport and Prusak draw the line between two kinds of knowing. Explicit knowledge is the kind that can be written down: a procedure, a fact, a definition. Tacit knowledge resists being transferred into a document or database, because so much of what makes it useful is the situation that surrounded it.

As Davenport and Prusak observe, such knowledge “incorporates so much accrued and embedded learning that its rules may be impossible to separate from how an individual acts.” When tacit knowledge is codified, it is necessarily transformed. The richness of the original is reduced to what can be represented in the system’s structural categories. The resulting representation is shaped by the categories the system makes available, the vocabulary it employs, and the hierarchical relationships it encodes.

Every codification decision involves what to preserve, what to simplify, and what to lose.

Consider how this plays out in practice. A support engineer resolves an issue for a customer and possesses, in that moment, a small piece of tacit knowledge — not only what fixed the problem but why, what they ruled out along the way, and how they would recognize the same issue if it surfaced differently in the future. Asked to document the fix, the engineer must reduce that experience to a written article. They decide which steps to include and which to skip. They pick a title that names the problem in language other engineers will recognize. They place the article in a category, perhaps Authentication, perhaps Network Errors, perhaps Common Issues. Each of those decisions is a rhetorical act. These decisions occur before information is organized into navigation structures or search experiences, but they determine what concepts exist, how they are named, and what relationships can later be expressed through the architecture.

Design Implications

By the time a user lands on a help article or a documentation page, a long chain of choices has already shaped what they can find. What knowledge got captured. What it was called. Which other concepts it was placed near. These choices live inside the codification layer, where the system’s vocabulary is set.

The categories you create, and the words you use to name them, will be inherited by every navigation menu, search result, and recommendation the system ever produces. Codification creates the raw material. IA arranges it. The next section, structural models, looks at how that arrangement gets done and what each option does to the user’s experience.

Key Terms

Codification The process of making tacit knowledge explicit and accessible through structured representation.
Tacit Knowledge Know-how and experiential understanding that resists full explicit representation.
Taxonomy A hierarchical classification system that encodes organizational values and shapes what can be known.
