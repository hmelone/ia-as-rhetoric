Part Three

Structural Models: Hierarchy, Facets, and Rhetorical Choice

Codification decides what concepts a system contains. Structural models decide how those concepts relate. Most knowledge systems run on one of two models, or a blend, and the choice shapes what readers can find, in what order, and with what sense of authority.

There are two dominant structural models. A hierarchy sends readers down a single path, from category to subcategory to page, giving each item one parent and one home. A faceted structure tags each item with several attributes the reader can filter and combine, so the same item can be reached from many directions.

An online store provides a great example. Browse strictly by hierarchy and you follow a fixed route, something like Clothing › Men’s › Shirts › Casual Shirts. A faceted structure lets the same shopper filter by brand, color, size, and price at once, reaching that shirt from any angle. What changes is the path to it, and the relationships the shopper sees along the way.

The Rhetoric of Hierarchy

Hierarchical organization works through parent-child relationships, each item getting one parent and one position. Content placed high reads as important or general; content buried deep reads as specific or secondary. The tree makes that argument whether the designer intends it or not. That is logos in action, the shape itself asserting a logic about how the subject fits together.

This works well when content genuinely has one home. A password-reset article belongs cleanly under Account Settings. A reference page for an API endpoint sits naturally under that API. When the single right home exists, a hierarchy reads as orderly and confident, and its logos holds up.

It breaks down when content has more than one reasonable home. A page on salmonella could sit under Diseases, Outbreak News, or Surveillance Data. Forcing one placement severs the other two relationships, and a reader approaching from the wrong direction will not find it. When the structure’s logic stops matching how readers actually think about the content, the system loses credibility. Its ethos slips.

Taxonomies in Practice

A taxonomy is a controlled vocabulary for classifying things. It can be a flat list of approved terms, or it can let an item live under several parents, but the most common form in a knowledge system is the hierarchical taxonomy, a named tree of concepts, each nested under one parent. If you have ever picked a category for an article or assigned a tag from a fixed list, you have used one.

A taxonomy does not describe how concepts really relate; it encodes how your organization chooses to relate them. It is often treated as if it reveals the natural structure of a domain, but in practice it reflects organizational priorities, assumptions, and goals. Dalkir is blunt about this: an organizational taxonomy expresses one company’s working concepts, not a description of reality that holds for everyone. Two organizations can classify the same content differently and each remain internally consistent. Taxonomies are interpretations, not discoveries.

That has practical fallout. The names you pick shape how readers search. The categories you create shape what content gets written. The point where you split one concept into two children shapes which distinctions feel meaningful to everyone who uses the system after you. Even at small scale, a taxonomy is a structural choice that carries rhetorical weight.

A Healthcare Site That Outgrew Its Hierarchy

Ruzza and colleagues studied the website of a large Italian healthcare organization, which forced everything from stable institutional pages to news, surveillance data, and disease information through a single hierarchy. Users reported that paths were too long and navigation was not intuitive, while the web team found related content scattered across different branches of the tree.

Readers experienced this as confusion. Tasks took longer, but the deeper problem was that people could no longer interpret how the system was organized at all. In the terms of Part One, the logos had stopped being legible.

Ruzza and colleagues did not abandon hierarchy. Instead they proposed a hybrid. A strict hierarchy would carry the stable institutional content, paired with a faceted classification for material that needs to live in several places at once. Hierarchy for what has a single home, facets for everything else.

The move that makes the hybrid work is linking the two. A news article tagged with a metadata facet surfaces automatically on the matching hierarchy page. Someone on the disease page sees recent outbreak news there; someone in the news section can filter to that disease. Both systems describe the same content, and neither has to own it outright.

How Facets Distribute Interpretive Control

The store example earlier is the everyday version of facets. Each item carries multiple attributes, like brand, color, size, and price, and the reader filters and combines them to arrive from any direction. In a knowledge system, the facets might be product area, audience, document type, or topic. The reader decides which ones matter for the question at hand and builds an individual path.

Why Facets Change the Rhetoric

The significance of facets runs past efficiency. Hierarchy and facets distribute interpretive control in opposite ways, which makes the choice between them a choice about who determines meaning: the designer or the reader. Hierarchy privileges a single path; the designer’s view of how knowledge relates becomes the only route through it, and the system’s logos stays singular and fixed. Facets hand that control to the reader. Each person assembles a sequence that fits their own need, so the logos turns plural. The system now supports several competing but valid arguments about how the content fits together, instead of asserting one.

This shifts the system’s ethos too. A faceted system makes no claim to be the only valid arrangement. By offering several honest paths to the same content, it reads as more trustworthy to people who arrive with different mental models. No one is told their way of thinking is wrong. Back in the healthcare case, a clinician can reach salmonella through the disease page, the surveillance data, or the regional outbreak news, and every route foregrounds a different set of relationships without hiding the rest.

The pattern generalizes well beyond healthcare. A software administrator might reach the same topic through a product area, a troubleshooting workflow, or a security concern, with each path surfacing different connections among the same underlying content.

The Limits of Faceted Models

Facets are no cure-all. Ruzza and colleagues are candid about the trade-off. Faceted classification rests on predetermined facets and controlled vocabularies, which means it cannot handle genuinely heterogeneous or ambiguous material. The categories must be defined up front, and those definitions encode organizational priorities, exactly as a hierarchy does.

So the flexibility is real but bounded. Readers can travel many paths, but only the paths the architecture already built. Facets do not remove rhetorical influence; they relocate it. The designer still decides which facets exist, how they are defined, and which relationships a reader is allowed to see.

Ontologies and the Semantic Layer

So far, hierarchies, taxonomies, and facets have all organized content for human readers. As information systems become increasingly machine-readable, information architecture extends beyond taxonomies and navigation into semantic models such as ontologies. Where a taxonomy classifies information into categories, an ontology defines the relationships between concepts and entities. A taxonomy can file salmonella under Diseases; an ontology can also encode that it spreads through contaminated food, produces particular symptoms, and responds to specific treatments.

These relationships reflect particular interpretations of how a domain should be understood. In this sense, ontologies extend the rhetorical work of information architecture from classification into knowledge representation itself. By determining what concepts exist and how they connect, they shape both human understanding and machine interpretation of information.

What This Means for Your Work

The case for hybrid models is practical and rhetorical at the same time. Letting readers approach content from different directions acknowledges that they arrive with different questions. A strict hierarchy makes one set of relationships the default for everyone, needed or not.

The useful skill is knowing which content wants which treatment. Stable, single-home material does well in a fixed hierarchy. Cross-cutting material needs faceted flexibility. Making that call deliberately, instead of defaulting to one model for everything, is most of the job.

Step back and the deeper point comes into view. Hierarchy, taxonomy, facets, and ontology are not just techniques for organizing information. Each is a way of expressing what a domain is, a claim about which relationships matter and which paths through the material make sense. No structural model is inevitable or universally correct. Each privileges certain relationships, assumptions, and paths through a domain while obscuring others.

Structure shapes interpretation when it works. Its rhetorical role becomes even clearer when it fails. The next section looks at what happens when readers can no longer follow a system’s logic, and why that failure tends to register as lost trust rather than a simple usability problem.

Key Terms

Hierarchical Classification A parent-child organization that assigns meaning by position within a fixed tree structure.
Faceted Classification A system associating content with multiple metadata categories, enabling multiple navigational pathways.
Ontology A semantic model that defines the relationships between concepts, shaping how both people and machines interpret a domain.
