Part Three

Structural Models: Hierarchy, Facets, and Rhetorical Choice

If structure shapes meaning, then the kind of structure you choose matters as much as whether you have one. Most web-based knowledge systems run on one of two models, or a mix of both. The choice between them shapes what readers can find, in what order, and with what sense of authority.

While codification determines what concepts a system can express, structural models determine how those concepts relate to one another. Two structural models dominate web-based knowledge systems. A hierarchy asks users to follow a single path through information: category, subcategory, subcategory, page. Each piece of content has one parent and one location. A faceted structure allows information to be discovered through multiple paths simultaneously, with each piece of content described by several attributes that the user can filter and combine.

A familiar example clarifies the difference. On an online store, browsing strictly by hierarchy would force a shopper to follow one path: Clothing › Men’s › Shirts › Casual Shirts. A faceted structure lets the same shopper filter by brand, color, size, and price all at once and arrive at the same shirt from any direction. The shirt has not changed. What changes is the path users take to reach it and the relationships they perceive between products.

The Rhetoric of Hierarchy

Hierarchical organization structures knowledge through parent-child relationships. Each item has one parent and one position in the tree. Content placed high in the hierarchy reads as more important or more general. Content buried in subsections reads as specific, secondary, or harder to reach. The hierarchy is doing rhetorical work whether the designer means it to or not.

That works well for content that genuinely fits one place. A help article about resetting a password might belong cleanly under Account Settings. A reference page about a specific API endpoint sits naturally under that API’s section. When the content actually does have a single right home, a hierarchy reads as orderly and confident.

It breaks down when the content has more than one reasonable home. A page about salmonella could plausibly sit under Diseases, under Outbreak News, or under Surveillance Data. A taxonomy that forces a single placement loses the other relationships. A reader who comes looking from the wrong direction will not find it.

Taxonomies: A Closer Look at Hierarchy

The most common form of hierarchy in a knowledge system is a taxonomy: a named tree of concepts, each one nested under a parent. If you have ever picked a category for a help article or assigned a tag from a controlled list, you have worked with a taxonomy.

The thing to understand as a practitioner is that a taxonomy is not a description of how concepts really relate. It is a decision about how your organization wants them to relate. Dalkir is direct about this: the purpose of an organizational taxonomy is not to describe reality universally, but to express the working concepts of a particular company. Different companies could build different taxonomies for the same content and both would be defensible. The taxonomy reflects priorities, not facts.

Taxonomy design has practical consequences. The names you pick will shape how readers search. The categories you create will shape what content gets written. The places where you split a concept into two children will shape which distinctions feel meaningful to everyone who works with the system after you. A taxonomy is a structural choice with rhetorical weight, even on the smallest scale.

A Case in Point: The Italian Healthcare Site

Ruzza and his coauthors documented what happens when a complex organization tries to run heterogeneous content through a single hierarchy. The site was the institutional website of a large Italian healthcare organization, with content ranging from stable institutional pages to dynamic news, surveillance data, and disease information. Users complained that “paths to information are too long” and navigation was “not intuitive.” Internally, the web team observed that related content was separated across different branches of the hierarchy, creating inconsistencies “among users both inside and outside the organization.”

Users experienced the mismatch as confusion. It caused a breakdown not just in efficiency, but in their ability to interpret the system’s organization at all.

Ruzza and his coauthors did not respond by rejecting hierarchy. They proposed a hybrid model: keep a strict hierarchy for stable institutional content, and pair it with a faceted classification system for content that needs to live in more than one place at once. The hierarchy carries the parts of the site that genuinely have a single home. The facets handle everything else.

The design move that makes the hybrid work is linking the two systems together. A news article tagged with a particular metadata facet automatically surfaces on the corresponding page in the hierarchy. A reader browsing the disease page sees recent outbreak news there. A reader browsing the news section can filter to that disease. The hierarchy and the facets describe the same content, but neither has to claim sole ownership of it.

Faceted Classification: Distributing Interpretive Control

The shopping example from earlier is the everyday version of facets. Each piece of content carries multiple attributes — brand, color, size, price — that the user can filter and combine to arrive at it from different directions. In a knowledge system, those facets might be product area, audience, document type, or topic. The reader picks which facets matter for their question and assembles their own path.

Rhetorical Significance of Faceted Structures

The rhetorical significance of facets goes beyond efficiency. Hierarchy and facets distribute interpretive control differently. Hierarchy privileges a single interpretive path. The designer’s view of how knowledge relates becomes the only available route through it. Faceted classification distributes that control. Users construct their own navigational sequences based on their particular needs, questions, or contexts.

A healthcare professional looking for information on salmonella might approach through the disease pathway, the surveillance data pathway, or the regional outbreak news pathway. Each route yields a different configuration of content, foregrounding different relationships. The system is not pretending to be neutral. It is letting different users build different paths through the same content.

The same dynamic appears in many knowledge systems. A software administrator might approach a topic through a product area, a troubleshooting workflow, or a security concern, each pathway revealing different relationships between the same information.

The Limits of Faceted Models

Ruzza et al.’s model has its own constraints. Faceted classification, they acknowledge, “is based on predetermined hierarchies of facets and controlled vocabularies; consequently, they cannot be used to manage heterogeneous or ambiguous information.” The metadata categories must be defined in advance, and those definitions encode organizational priorities.

The system’s interpretive flexibility is real but limited. Users can navigate across multiple paths, but only along the paths the architecture makes available. Facets do not eliminate rhetorical influence. They simply move it. Designers still decide which facets exist, how they are defined, and which relationships users are allowed to see.

Design Implications

The case for hybrid structural models is practical and rhetorical at once. A system that lets users approach content from different directions acknowledges that different users come with different questions. A strict hierarchy makes one set of relationships the default for everyone, whether they need it or not.

Designers who understand this can make more deliberate choices about which content benefits from fixed hierarchical placement and which benefits from faceted flexibility.

Structural models shape interpretation when they work. Their rhetorical role becomes even more visible when they fail.
