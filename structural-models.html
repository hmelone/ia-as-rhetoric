Part Three

Structural Models and Rhetorical Choice

Codification determines what concepts a knowledge system contains. Structural models determine how those concepts relate to one another. Whether a system relies on hierarchy, facets, or a combination of both influences not only what readers can find, but how they understand the relationships between ideas. Structure does not simply organize information. It presents an interpretation of the domain itself.

There are two dominant structural models. A hierarchy sends readers down a single path, from category to subcategory to page, giving each item one parent and one home. A faceted structure describes each item through several attributes that readers can filter and combine, so the same item can be reached from many directions.

An online store provides a simple example. Browse strictly by hierarchy and you follow a fixed route, something like Clothing › Men’s › Shirts › Casual Shirts. A faceted structure lets the same shopper filter by brand, color, size, and price at once, reaching that shirt from several directions. The product never changes. What changes is the structure surrounding it, and with it the relationships the shopper sees.

The Rhetoric of Hierarchy

Hierarchical organization works through parent-child relationships, with each item assigned one parent and one position. Content placed high reads as important or general. Content buried deep reads as specific or secondary. The tree itself makes that argument whether the designer intends it or not. That is logos in action, with the structure asserting a logic about how the subject fits together.

This works well when content genuinely has one home. A password-reset article belongs cleanly under Account Settings. A reference page for an API endpoint sits naturally under that API. When the single right home exists, a hierarchy reads as orderly and confident, and its logos holds up.

It breaks down when content has more than one reasonable home. A page on salmonella could sit under Diseases, Outbreak News, or Surveillance Data. Forcing one placement severs the other two relationships, and a reader approaching from the wrong direction will not find it. When the structure’s logic stops matching how readers actually think about the content, the system loses credibility. Its ethos slips.

Taxonomies Make Hierarchy Possible

Every hierarchy depends on a taxonomy. Before information can be arranged into parent-child relationships, someone has to decide which concepts exist, what they are called, and how they relate to one another. Those decisions create the vocabulary that gives a hierarchy its structure.

A taxonomy is that vocabulary. In a knowledge system, it often appears as a hierarchy of named concepts, with each term nested beneath a broader parent. If you have ever assigned an article to a product, topic, audience, or issue type from a controlled list, you have worked with a taxonomy.

That vocabulary does not reveal the natural structure of a domain. It reflects how an organization chooses to understand it. As Dalkir argues, an organizational taxonomy expresses one company’s working concepts rather than a universal description of reality. Two organizations can classify the same content differently and both remain internally consistent.

That makes taxonomy rhetorical. The categories you create influence what content gets written. The names you choose influence how readers search. The point where one concept becomes two influences which distinctions feel meaningful to everyone who uses the system after you. A taxonomy does not simply organize knowledge. It expresses an interpretation of the domain.

How Facets Distribute Interpretive Control

The store example earlier is the everyday version of facets. Each item carries multiple attributes, such as brand, color, size, and price, and the reader filters and combines them to arrive from more than one direction. In a knowledge system, the facets might be product area, audience, document type, task, issue type, or topic. The reader decides which attributes matter for the question at hand and builds an individual path through the system.

Why Facets Change the Rhetoric

The significance of facets runs past efficiency. Hierarchy and facets distribute interpretive control in opposite ways, which makes the choice between them a choice about who determines meaning. A hierarchy privileges a single path through the content. The designer’s view of how knowledge relates becomes the primary route through it, and the system’s logos stays singular and fixed.

Facets shift some of that control to the reader. Each person assembles a path that fits their own need, so the logos turns plural. The system now supports several valid arguments about how the content fits together instead of asserting only one.

This shifts the system’s ethos too. A faceted system makes no claim to be the only valid arrangement. By offering several honest paths to the same content, it reads as more trustworthy to people who arrive with different mental models. No one is told their way of thinking is wrong.

The pattern generalizes across knowledge systems. A software administrator might reach the same topic through a product area, a troubleshooting workflow, or a security concern, with each path surfacing different connections among the same underlying content.

A Hybrid Model in Practice

The value of a hybrid model becomes clearer when a hierarchy is asked to represent information that does not fit neatly into one path. Ruzza and colleagues studied the website of a large Italian healthcare organization, which forced everything from stable institutional pages to news, surveillance data, and disease information through a single hierarchy. Users reported that paths were too long and navigation was not intuitive, while the web team found related content scattered across different branches of the tree.

The deeper problem was not simply that tasks took longer. Readers could no longer interpret how the system itself was organized. In the terms of Part One, the logos had stopped being legible.

Ruzza and colleagues did not abandon hierarchy. Instead, they proposed a hybrid model. A strict hierarchy would carry the stable institutional content, paired with a faceted classification for material that needs to live in several places at once. Hierarchy served the content with a single home. Facets served the content with multiple meaningful relationships.

The move that makes the hybrid work is linking the two. A news article tagged with a metadata facet surfaces automatically on the matching hierarchy page. Someone on the disease page sees recent outbreak news there, while someone in the news section can filter to that disease. The same content becomes available through multiple meaningful relationships without forcing the system to privilege only one of them.

The Limits of Faceted Models

Facets are no cure-all. Ruzza and colleagues are candid about the tradeoff. Faceted classification rests on predetermined facets and controlled vocabularies, which means it cannot handle genuinely heterogeneous or ambiguous material without prior modeling. The categories must be defined up front, and those definitions encode organizational priorities, exactly as a hierarchy does.

So the flexibility is real but bounded. Readers can travel many paths, but only the paths the architecture already built. Facets do not remove rhetorical influence. They relocate it. The designer still decides which facets exist, how they are defined, and which relationships a reader is allowed to see.

Ontologies and the Semantic Layer

The structural models discussed so far primarily shape the paths through which readers encounter information. Hierarchies establish broader and narrower relationships. Facets let readers combine multiple attributes. Ontologies and knowledge graphs extend this work by representing more varied relationships among concepts, entities, and information resources.

An ontology defines the concepts within a domain and the types of relationships that can connect them. A taxonomy can classify salmonella under Diseases. An ontology can additionally establish that diseases may be transmitted through particular sources, produce particular symptoms, affect particular populations, and respond to particular treatments.

A knowledge graph applies these relationships to actual entities and information. It might connect salmonella to contaminated food, fever, outbreak reports, prevention guidance, and treatment information. These connections can power semantic search, related-content recommendations, exploratory navigation, data integration, and visual representations of a subject.

This semantic layer serves both people and machines. It helps people discover information through meaningful relationships rather than exact keywords or a single navigational path. It helps machines distinguish entities, interpret context, connect information across systems, and retrieve more relevant material for search and AI applications.

These models are still rhetorical. An ontology determines which entities and relationships the system can recognize. A knowledge graph makes particular connections visible while leaving others absent. Together, they shape what people and machines can find, how they understand the domain, and which relationships appear meaningful or authoritative.

What This Means for Your Work

The case for hybrid models is practical and rhetorical at the same time. Letting readers approach content from different directions acknowledges that they arrive with different questions. A strict hierarchy makes one set of relationships the default for everyone, whether or not that structure matches the reader’s need.

The challenge is recognizing which structural model best fits the knowledge being represented. Stable, single-home content often benefits from hierarchy. Cross-cutting information benefits from facets. More complex knowledge systems may need semantic relationships that taxonomies alone cannot express. Choosing deliberately rather than defaulting to one model is itself an architectural decision.

Hierarchy, taxonomy, facets, and ontology are not just techniques for organizing information. Each is a way of expressing what a domain is, a claim about which relationships matter and which paths through the material make sense. No structural model is inevitable or universally correct. Each privileges certain relationships, assumptions, and paths while obscuring others.

Structure shapes interpretation when it works. Its rhetorical role becomes even clearer when it fails. The next section looks at what happens when readers can no longer follow a system’s logic, and why that failure tends to register as lost trust rather than a simple usability problem.
